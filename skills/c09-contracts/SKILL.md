# Deal Control — Paper and the Calendar

Two things run a contract: the paper and the dates. Each is binary — working or not, nothing in between.

The framing worth planting early: **if the dollar is not papered and the deadline is not calendared, they do not have a deal. They have a mood.**

## The Friday audit — fifteen minutes

Every open contract date for the next ninety days, in one place. LOI expirations, due-diligence ends, contingency deadlines, key lease dates, closings.

Three questions, in order:

1. **Which dates moved this week?** Extensions negotiated, closings rescheduled, diligence extended. Reality first.
2. **What is inside its warning window?** Anything approaching gets a next action assigned now, with a name against it.
3. **Which dollars are still naked?** Every expected commission without executed paper.

**If they cannot complete the list, that is the finding.** A failed audit does not mean they are bad at Fridays. It means their deadlines currently exist as vibes, which means the next casualty is already sitting in their future, chosen and unnamed.

Say that plainly. The audit failing is the alarm, not the embarrassment.

## Naked dollars

Total every expected commission this quarter with no signed paper naming them.

That number is GCI they are *believing* rather than *holding*. For each one, name the single action that papers it: send the agreement, schedule the exclusivity conversation, or move it off the expectations list and back into nurture where it belongs.

## Where deadlines die

An inbox is a river — everything flows past once and anything not acted on immediately is functionally gone. A calendar is a structure. Dates persist and announce themselves whether or not anyone remembers why they matter.

The difference between advisors who keep their closings and advisors who donate them back is often nothing more than which container the deadline lived in.

When a deal dies on a missed date, the cause is never laziness. It is architecture: **the deadline was stored in a document instead of a system.** Documents inform. Systems alert.

## The distinction worth drawing

Construction solved this by making the date cost money. A liquidated damages clause sets a dollar figure per day of delay, calculated at signing from the owner's documented daily losses, and it applies automatically — the federal contracting regulation says the contractor *shall pay* for each day until the work is accepted.

Watch what that does to a jobsite. Every schedule becomes real. Every change order gets papered immediately, because an unpapered delay is a delay you eat.

An advisor's dates are just as real and have no mechanism attached. Miss one and there is no automatic anything — there is a phone call from a position of weakness, and an extension they now have to ask for instead of grant. **They do not get that clause.** Which means the watching a liquidated damages clause forces on a jobsite is watching they have to do themselves, or nobody does.

## Paper — what counts

**Counts:** signed listing agreements, signed tenant representation agreements, executed purchase agreements with commission protection intact, signed exclusivity letters, fee agreements naming them on anything outside standard forms.

**Does not count:** verbal confirmations, emails describing an intention, handshake terms, and — this one catches teams — paper naming the *company* without protecting *them* under their split.

The question to ask of anything they sign: *when this closes, does money route to me by contract, or by goodwill?*

**The gray zone:** sometimes a client genuinely cannot sign today — procurement, board approval, legal review. Fine. No substantial work product leaves until paper is *in motion*, with a named signer and a date. If it has been "pending" for three Fridays running, it is not pending.

## Two boundaries

**Negotiation belongs to the advisor accountable to the client.** Preparation delegates. Defined issues can go to qualified team members — a senior broker taking the estoppel while they take price is normal practice. Counsel negotiates language. What does not delegate is strategy and the final trade-offs: what they give, what they will not, and when they walk.

**Calendar tracking does delegate.** A capable assistant maintains the list. Deciding what an approaching deadline requires does not, because acting on a deadline is negotiating.

## The team layer

If they run deals with a team or inside a brokerage, ask a harder question: is their name on the client relationship while their money rides on internal goodwill?

Splits in writing. Referral fees documented before the introduction, not after the check arrives. Co-listing arrangements papered while everyone is friendly — documentation drafted during goodwill is cheap, and the same document after a dispute is litigation.

And when they are the senior party, write it down first anyway. Advisors remember who paid cleanly and who had to be chased.

## The workbook rule

If the advisor uses the companion workbook, **never write to the .xlsx file.**

Its formulas feed a dashboard across thirteen tabs, and a library will write straight through Excel's sheet protection without warning. A clobbered formula produces no error — it produces a wrong number that looks right.

Output a paste-ready block instead. Tell them which tab and which column, and let them paste it. That takes them four seconds and it cannot break anything.

If they explicitly ask you to write the file anyway, say what the risk is first, and tell them to run `verify_repair.py --repair` afterwards.
